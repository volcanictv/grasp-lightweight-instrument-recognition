Surgical Instrument Classification on GraSP

Status: 2026-09-03

What was set out to do

Given a surgical video frame from GraSP, identify which of 7 instruments is present (Bipolar Forceps, Prograsp Forceps, Large Needle Driver, Monopolar Curved Scissors, Suction Instrument, Clip Applier, Laparoscopic Grasper). This is scoped to classification only. Once accuracy was solid, whether the classifier had actually learned to recognize instruments, or just memorized GraSP's own footage, was also investigated. It was tested against surgical video it had never seen (EndoVis 2017 and 2018, two independent public datasets).

How it was approached

GraSP frames routinely contain 2-3 instruments at once, and the instrument mix is heavily imbalanced (the rarest class has ~25x fewer examples than the most common). Each instrument is classified individually, given its location, rather than trying to describe a whole frame at once. This alone resolved most of the rare-class problem. From there, model capacity and resolution were scaled up, a crop-preprocessing issue that was compressing an instrument's most distinctive part was fixed, and multiple trained models were combined into an ensemble. Every change was tested in isolation against a fixed baseline, changing one variable at a time to isolate exactly which change caused which result. Every reported number was also re-checked by retraining the full recipe from scratch on a separate held-out data split and re-evaluating there, not just scored against the same test set each time.

Rare-class performance

These two classes have very few test examples and are where earlier, simpler fixes tended to backfire: stronger class reweighting raised recall but collapsed precision, and cropping in tighter around just the instrument tip discarded context both classes needed. Both made results worse, not better.

Does it generalize beyond GraSP?

A model that only works on the exact dataset it was trained on isn't that useful. The classifier was run, completely unmodified and with no retraining, against two independent public surgical video datasets (EndoVis 2017 and 2018) that share part of GraSP's instrument set.

The three bulkiest, most visually distinct instruments transferred well (F1 0.45–0.73) on camera setups and instrument hardware the model had never seen. Two thinner instruments did not transfer on at least one of the two datasets. This was investigated and traced to hardware differences between datasets (a different instrument generation/manufacturer), not a flaw in the model: the same class that failed on one external dataset scored well on the other, with the identical unmodified classifier.

Efficiency

Whether the model could be made faster or smaller without losing accuracy was investigated, and the standard first technique (INT8 quantization) was tested directly rather than assumed to help. It made the model measurably slower on standard CPU hardware and cost real accuracy: 1-4 points off the two ResNet-50 models, and 63-74 points off the two lightweight models, which collapsed to near-random. Not adopted. The current model ships as-is; a cheaper replacement for one of the two ResNet-50 checkpoints requires new training.

Handoff

A clean, integration-ready package (the trained ensemble plus a single predict() call, no training or experiment code) is ready for another team to integrate against their own detection/localization pipeline: github.com/volcanictv/grasp-instrument-classifier.

Limitations, and what's being done about them

Laparoscopic Grasper vs. Suction Instrument. When this instrument's jaws are closed, it's a smooth, featureless shaft, visually identical to a suction tube in a single still frame. Four different approaches were ruled out: a narrow (~20-frame) and a wide (~200-frame, ~13-second) search across nearby video frames for a moment the jaws open, checking GraSP's own action-state labels as a non-visual signal, and a targeted fine-tune explicitly pushing the two classes' learned representations apart. None found a fix. This is a limitation of single-frame classification, not a training gap.

The ensemble's exact weighting is still being finalized. The current version favors its strongest model; a simpler, equally-tested alternative scores about 1 point lower overall and is available as a fallback.

Next steps

  1. Grasper/Suction confusion: collect and label additional training examples specifically covering this closed-jaw ambiguity.
  2. Efficiency: train a lighter backbone (ResNet-18 or EfficientNet-B0) as a drop-in replacement for one of the two ResNet-50 ensemble members, to test whether most of its accuracy contribution can be kept at a fraction of the compute cost.
