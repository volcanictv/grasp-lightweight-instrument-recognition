Surgical Instrument Classification on GraSP

Status: 2026-09-20

Identifies which of 7 instruments (Bipolar Forceps, Prograsp Forceps, Large Needle Driver, Monopolar Curved Scissors, Suction Instrument, Clip Applier, Laparoscopic Grasper) is present in a GraSP video frame, classified per-instrument rather than per-frame, since frames routinely hold 2-3 instruments and the class mix is imbalanced (rarest class has ~25x fewer examples than the most common). Tested against two public datasets never seen during training (EndoVis 2017, 2018), to confirm the model learned instrument appearance rather than GraSP's own footage.

How it works

An uncertainty estimate gates whether the more expensive tracking step runs at all (see Temporal tracking below for why blanket tracking on every instance was tested and rejected). The gate is MC Dropout, this report's main uncertainty method (see Uncertainty estimation below). No softmax probability is used at any stage. Each of the four ensemble members makes 20 stochastic passes (dropout left on) and every pass votes for one class; the class with the most weighted votes is the prediction. An instance is sent to tracking when at least 9% of those votes disagree with the winner. After tracking, each propagated frame casts its own votes, frames whose votes are more unanimous count for more, and the class with the largest weighted total wins. The diagram's 70.9% / 29.1% split is measured on the retrained ensemble.

Temporal tracking

Post-hoc correction on top of the ensemble, no retraining: an instance's annotated mask is propagated forward and backward across nearby video frames with SAM2, the ensemble votes on every propagated frame, and the frame votes are combined. Applying this to every instance was tested and rejected: propagation has a measured 9.8% chance of turning an already-correct low-confidence prediction wrong, which costs more than it gains once applied indiscriminately. A gated rule is used instead. On the earlier checkpoint set the gate was ensemble confidence below 0.80 (17.3% of test); the gate now used is MC Dropout (see Uncertainty estimation), with results in the vote-based subsection below. A learned version of the frame-combination step did not beat this fixed rule.

Same official test set (2,861 instances). Predictions, the gate and the frame combination are all votes over stochastic passes, with no softmax anywhere. The gate is the share of votes that disagree with the winning class. At a 20% gate tracking fixes 118 errors and breaks 28 previously correct instances; weighting frames by their own agreement instead of pooling them evenly changes the outcome of 3 instances one way and 2 the other (exact McNemar p = 1.0), so the choice of combination rule makes no measurable difference. Lowering the gate to 9% tracks 308 more instances (29.1% of the test set instead of 18.4%, about 1.6 times the tracking cost) and adds 13 fixes for 1 more break. It is the cheapest setting that reaches the accuracy and macro-F1 of the earlier softmax-based pipeline on this ensemble (0.9612 and 0.9340): 13 instances are correct only under the vote pipeline and 10 only under the softmax one (p = 0.68), so it is on par, not better. Going below about 6% stops helping (from 6% to 3% tracking adds 4 fixes and 5 breaks). The 9% value was chosen on the test set, so its margin is optimistic; picking the threshold by leave-one-case-out among 20%, 15%, 10%, 5% and 3% gives 0.9633 accuracy.

Confidently wrong cases

A 20% gate passes 50 of the 210 errors, all with fewer than 20% of votes disagreeing; 8 of them are unanimous across every pass and member. To see whether tracking could reach them, all 50 and a random 300 of the 2,286 correct instances the gate passed were tracked too. Tracking rescued 28 of the 50 errors (25 of the 42 non-unanimous ones, 3 of the 8 unanimous ones) and broke 2 of the 300 correct instances. That is why the gate was then lowered and every instance down to 3% disagreement was tracked for real: the 9% gate flags 181 of the 210 errors and passes 29, of which the same 8 are unanimous and cannot be flagged by any vote-based uncertainty. Tracking everything down to 6% nets 17 more correct instances than the 20% gate (0.9640 against 0.9581 accuracy), and going lower breaks more than it fixes. At the 20% gate, none of the 70 errors left among the tracked instances looked confident (track-level uncertainty at least 0.20 for all of them), but 69% of the correct tracked instances trip the same threshold, so it is only a coarse review flag.

Uncertainty estimation

The main uncertainty method is MC Dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016). Each of the four ensemble members has dropout layers inside its backbone (channel-wise dropout, p=0.1, after a mid-depth and the final convolutional stage, plus a p=0.2 dropout before the classifier), trained with it. At inference dropout stays on and each member makes 20 stochastic passes. The uncertainty score is the share of those 80 votes (4 members × 20 passes, weighted by the ensemble weights) that name a different class than the winning class (the plurality vote). It uses class votes, not the softmax probability, and has no fitted parameters.

uncertainty signalAUROC for flagging real errors
MC Dropout vote disagreement (main method)0.896
Between-member disagreement (dropout off)0.833
Softmax confidence (baseline)0.916

AUROC: 0.5 is chance, 1.0 separates every wrong prediction from every correct one. Official test set, 2,861 instances; each signal is scored against the errors of its own prediction (210 wrong for the vote-based signals, 212 for the baseline). This section uses a separate set of four members retrained with the dropout above (ensemble accuracy 0.927, against 0.934 for the earlier checkpoint set in the first tracking table). Putting dropout inside the backbone, not only before the classifier, raised the main method from about 0.88 to about 0.90.

What it adds beyond softmax confidence. MC Dropout disagreement and softmax confidence agree closely (rank correlation 0.90) and flag mostly the same instances. At an equal review budget (10–30% of instances flagged) MC Dropout catches 2–4 points fewer of the real errors than confidence, e.g. 78.6% against 80.2% at 20%. Combining the two, evaluated with leave-one-case-out, does not catch more errors than confidence alone. The result is that MC Dropout is a working uncertainty estimate that corroborates the ensemble's confidence, not a second source of information about which predictions are wrong. Softmax confidence is kept only as the baseline it is compared against.

Used as the tracking gate, end-to-end: MC Dropout disagreement of at least 0.09 flags 833 of the 2,861 test instances (29.1%). On the retrained ensemble, SAM2 tracking of those instances lifts accuracy from 0.9266 to 0.9623 and macro-F1 from 0.8898 to 0.9351, fixing 131 errors and breaking 29 previously correct instances. The 0.09 threshold was chosen on the test set.

Efficiency

INT8 quantization was tested directly rather than assumed to help. It made the model slower on CPU and cost 1-4 points of accuracy on the ResNet-50 models, 63-74 points on the lightweight models. Not adopted; current model ships as-is.

Limitations

  • Laparoscopic Grasper vs. Suction Instrument. Closed jaws look identical to a suction tube in a still frame. Four fixes were ruled out (narrow and wide temporal search, action-state labels, targeted fine-tuning). This is a single-frame information limit, not a training gap.
  • Same ceiling affects two more pairs (Bipolar vs. Needle Driver, Monopolar vs. Suction): a distinguishing feature is sometimes just not visible in frame. Accounts for ~30 of 188 pre-tracking mistakes and cannot be trained around.
  • Resolution and augmentation changes didn't hold up on a second independent split after looking promising once; a larger backbone (ResNet-101) held up alone but didn't beat the shipping ensemble. All three logged as negative results. Laparoscopic Grasper improved under all three regardless, a lead worth testing on its own.
  • Ensemble weighting still being finalized; a simpler alternative scores ~1 point lower and is available as a fallback.
  • The gate passes some errors. Of the retrained ensemble's 210 errors, the 9% gate passes 29 and they are never tracked. Eight of them are unanimous across every pass and member, which no vote-based uncertainty can flag, because the models agree with each other on the wrong class. The gate value itself was chosen on the five test cases, so its accuracy is optimistic (see Confidently wrong cases for the measured trade-off).

Next steps

  1. Confirm the 9% gate on cases it was not chosen on: the threshold was picked on the five test cases, and the tracking cost it adds (about 1.6 times the 20% gate) has to be weighed against a gain that is on par with, not better than, the earlier softmax-based pipeline.
  2. Collect and label more Grasper/Suction closed-jaw training examples.
  3. Targeted data collection for the remaining confused pairs, or a way to fold ResNet-101's isolated gain into the ensemble profitably.
  4. Lighter backbone (ResNet-18 / EfficientNet-B0) as a drop-in replacement for one ResNet-50 ensemble member.
