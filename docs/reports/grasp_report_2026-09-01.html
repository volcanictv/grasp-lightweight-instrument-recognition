Lightweight Surgical Instrument Recognition on GraSP

Can a small, cheap neural network hold its own against a much bigger, slower one at recognizing surgical instruments in prostate-surgery video?

Good news so far: our small, cheap model beats a much bigger one on both speed and accuracy for box-drawing — not a tradeoff, just better. §1 Abstract

The question this report answers: can a small, cheap neural network (MobileNetV3, a model built for phones) recognize surgical instruments in the GraSP dataset (video of robot-assisted prostate surgery) nearly as well as a big, expensive one? We test this four different ways — "what instruments are in this frame," "what instrument is this cropped picture of," "draw a box around each instrument," and "color in each instrument's exact pixels" — getting more precise (and harder) each time.

The headline result: fine-tuning the small model (letting it adjust to this specific dataset, not just using it off-the-shelf) makes a big difference for plain classification. Cropping to one instrument at a time, instead of asking "what's somewhere in this whole frame," fixes almost every remaining weak spot. And for drawing boxes, the small model actually beats a much bigger one (ResNet-50) on both accuracy and speed — not a tradeoff, a clean win.

The one recurring problem

Every box-drawing model we tried gets noticeably worse when instruments overlap on screen — it loses 25–30 points of recall (its ability to find the instrument at all) on heavily-overlapping instruments compared to ones sitting alone. We tried two fixes: a simple frame-to-frame tracker (helped a little, not enough to call a win) and a from-scratch pixel-coloring model (found and fixed a real bug, nearly doubled its main score).

We also checked how these numbers stack up against the researchers who built this dataset and published their own model (TAPIS) alongside it. Our box-drawing model is within about 5–9 points of theirs. Our pixel-coloring model is within about 15 points on one measure, but a much wider 52 points on the measure that actually matters most for that task — we tracked that gap down to us using a different (and, for that specific measure, weaker) kind of architecture, and we're now building the stronger kind instead, reusing the box-drawing model we already trained as a head start.

§2 Introduction

Knowing which surgical instrument is on screen, and where, is useful for reviewing how an operation went, training surgeons, and eventually letting software assist in real time. The obvious way to do this well is a big, powerful model — TAPIS, the model published alongside the GraSP dataset, is exactly that. But big models are slow and memory-hungry, which is a real problem if you want this running live in an operating room. So the question here is narrower and more practical: how much of that accuracy can a small, cheap model keep, and how much faster/lighter does it get in exchange? We're not proposing a new architecture — just honestly measuring that tradeoff.

One wrinkle: almost every frame in GraSP has more than one instrument in it (94.7% of them do). That rules out the simplest version of this problem ("name the one instrument in this picture") — it has to be some form of "name all of them" or "find each one separately." We tried four versions, each pinning down location more precisely than the last:

  • Task A — "what instruments are somewhere in this frame": the model outputs a yes/no confidence for each of the 7 instrument types, independently.
  • Task B — "what instrument is this cropped picture of": we cut out just the pixels belonging to one instrument (using its known outline) and ask the model to name that one instrument.
  • Detection — "draw a box around every instrument and label it": the standard object-detection setup, scored the same way every published GraSP benchmark scores it, so our numbers are directly comparable to theirs. We also tried adding a lightweight tracker on top, to use the fact that video frames come in sequence.
  • Instance segmentation — "color in every instrument's exact pixels": the most precise (and hardest) version, and the one built specifically to attack a problem we found in the detection stage (below).

§3 Related work

The GraSP dataset paper (Ayobi et al., "Pixel-Wise Recognition for Holistic Surgical Scene Understanding," arXiv 2401.11174; Medical Image Analysis, 2025) is where this dataset comes from. It also introduces TAPIS, a large transformer model that combines whole-video context with per-instrument region proposals. TAPIS reports one overall accuracy number (89.85%, a segmentation-style score explained in §4.4) with no per-instrument breakdown, and doesn't do anything special to handle the fact that some instruments are much rarer than others — a gap this project documents independently in §4.1.

Nobody else treats GraSP as a plain classification problem the way Task A/B do here — every other group scores it as a detection or segmentation task. We separately checked the published literature on handling overlapping objects (notes in docs/detection_literature_notes.md): "paste extra copies of rare objects into training images" (Ghiasi et al. 2021) and "track objects frame-to-frame so a brief detection miss doesn't lose them" (Bochinski et al. 2017) are both well-established, low-cost tricks, not anything new we invented. Segmenting instruments without relying on boxes at all (Kurmann et al. 2021; the "Panoptic-DeepLab" family of models) is the standard approach for exactly the overlapping-instrument problem this report runs into.

§4 Methods

4.1Dataset

GraSP has video from 13 real prostate surgeries, split by the dataset's own authors into 8 for training and 5 for testing (we never touch that split ourselves — using someone else's fixed split keeps our numbers honest and comparable). That gives 2324 labeled training frames and 1125 labeled test frames, with 6170 and 2861 individual instrument instances respectively, across 7 instrument types. Some instrument types are much rarer than others: Clip Applier appears only 102 times total, vs. 2609 times for Monopolar Curved Scissors — a 25x difference, which is the main challenge classification has to deal with.

4.2Models

For classification, we compare five off-the-shelf backbones, all pretrained on ImageNet (a large general-purpose photo dataset, the standard starting point): MobileNetV3-Small (our proposed lightweight choice), MobileNetV3-Large, EfficientNet-B0, ResNet-18, and ResNet-50 (used deliberately as the "big and expensive" comparison point). For box-drawing (detection), we use Faster R-CNN, a standard two-stage detector, again in a small version (18.9M parameters — "parameters" are the model's tunable numbers, roughly a proxy for size and cost) and a big version (41.1M parameters). For pixel-coloring (segmentation), there's no reliable off-the-shelf lightweight option, so we built one: a small network that predicts, per pixel, which instrument class it belongs to, plus a second small head that helps separate touching instruments into individual instances. We built two versions of this network's backbone — the first only fed it shallow, low-detail features, which turned out to be a real bug (§5.7); the fixed version also uses deeper, richer features and is still fairly small (5.9M parameters).

4.3Training setup

When fine-tuning (letting a pretrained model adjust its weights to this dataset), the newly-added head layer learns faster than the pretrained backbone, since the backbone already knows something useful and shouldn't be disrupted too much. Task A's best setup combines two things: giving rare instrument classes more weight in the loss (so getting them wrong "costs" the model more), and stronger data augmentation (randomly flipping, recoloring, or cropping training images so the model sees more variety) — kept within what's physically plausible for a surgery video (flipping left-right is fine; flipping upside-down or shifting colors so tissue looks unnatural is not). Detection trains on full-size images and stops automatically once accuracy stops improving for a while ("early stopping"), rather than training for a fixed number of passes through the data. The tracking add-on isn't a trained model at all — it's a simple after-the-fact rule that links up boxes from nearby video frames, so a box that briefly vanishes (behind another instrument, say) can be bridged rather than lost; we added a couple of safety checks to it after an early version made results worse, not better (§5.6). The segmentation model predicts three things per pixel at once (which instrument, roughly where its center is, and a small position correction), each with its own loss term, added together.

4.4Evaluation

For Task A and Task B we report standard classification scores: how often the model is right, plus per-instrument precision (of the times it guessed an instrument, how often was it correct), recall (of the times an instrument was really there, how often did it get found), and F1 (a single number combining both). For detection, we use "mAP" — mean average precision, the standard box-detection score, which checks both whether a box is roughly in the right place (using an overlap threshold, called IoU) and whether it has the right label.

What "occlusion-stratified recall" actually means

This is the diagnostic we use throughout to answer "does the model struggle when instruments overlap?" In plain terms: for every real instrument in a frame, we measure how much of its box is covered by some other instrument's box. Then we sort instruments into three buckets — alone (no overlap), lightly covered (up to half), and heavily covered (more than half) — and separately check what fraction of each bucket the model actually finds. This is a rough proxy (two boxes can overlap on paper without either instrument visually hiding the other), and it's checked at one fixed confidence setting, not swept across many settings the way the headline mAP score is. That distinction matters: in §5.6, a change made this recall number look better while making the real mAP score worse.

For segmentation, we additionally report mIoU (mean intersection-over-union: on average, how much do the model's predicted pixels for an instrument overlap with the true pixels) and a couple of its standard variants, so our numbers line up with what other papers on this dataset report.

4.5Hardware & benchmarking

Training and speed testing used two older NVIDIA Titan Xp GPUs (server-grade cards, but an older generation without the newer speed tricks modern GPUs have). For classification models we report parameter count, file size, GPU speed, and CPU speed (via ONNX, a portable model format that runs without a GPU at all — a stand-in for "how fast would this run on a laptop or an embedded device"). For detection models we only report GPU speed; the CPU/ONNX numbers aren't reliable for this kind of two-stage detector, so we didn't fake a number just to fill the table.

§5.6 Tracking-by-detection

Our detector runs fast enough (~22ms a frame) to have room to spare under a real-time 33ms-per-frame budget, so we tried spending that spare time on something the detector alone can't do: use the fact that video frames come in sequence. If an instrument briefly gets missed for a frame or two — often because something else moved in front of it — a tracker can carry its last known position forward and "cover" for that gap, rather than the pipeline just losing it. We used a well-known, simple method called an IOU-Tracker: it links up boxes between consecutive frames if they overlap enough, and lets a track survive being missed for a few frames (a setting called max_age) before giving up on it.

First check — does it find more overlapping instruments? Yes: recall on heavily-overlapping instruments went from 0.645 up to as high as 0.683, and it went up a bit for the easier buckets too. That's a genuine improvement, and one a real-time system could actually use (it only ever looks backward in time, never forward).

Second check, the one that actually matters — does overall accuracy go up?

No, at least not at first: the very first version scored worse overall (0.7899) than not tracking at all (0.8264). Why: when a track "covers" for a missing instrument by guessing its last known box, that guess is sometimes wrong — either because the tracker was never confident about that instrument to begin with, or because the instrument didn't just get briefly hidden, it actually left the frame for good, and the tracker kept guessing a stale box anyway. We fixed both: don't let a low-confidence track guess at all, and if a track is near the frame's edge and moving further out, treat that as "it's gone," not "it's hidden."

We then tried different values for how long a track is allowed to guess before giving up (max_age): the shorter that window, the less time a guess has to go stale, and the better the score got. Letting a track guess for just one missed frame (max_age=1) worked best.

Tried and didn't help: smarter guessing about what's occluding what. We tried a fancier version that checks whether a missing instrument's predicted position actually overlaps another real, currently-detected instrument (i.e., "is something really blocking it"), tested three different ways — all of them scored a bit worse than the simple version above. Best guess why: GraSP frames almost always have 2-3 instruments in them anyway, so "there's another instrument box nearby" is true almost all the time, whether or not it's actually the thing blocking the view.

Bottom line, stated plainly

After fixing two real bugs and trying several variations, tracking's best version still scores slightly worse overall (1.34 points below not tracking at all), even though it does genuinely help find more overlapping instruments. It's cheap to run and the idea is sound, but as it stands it isn't a clear win.

§5.7 Instance segmentation

This model exists specifically to fix the overlapping-instrument problem detection couldn't. Standard box detectors have a cleanup step that deletes duplicate overlapping boxes, which can accidentally delete a real, heavily-overlapping instrument along with the duplicates. A model that skips boxes entirely and works pixel-by-pixel doesn't have that failure mode. Ours predicts, per pixel, which instrument class it belongs to, plus a rough "center point" heatmap per instrument that's used afterward to separate touching instruments into individual objects — a well-established approach (Kurmann et al. 2021; the Panoptic-DeepLab family), not something new we invented.

First attempt, and a bug in reading the output. The first training run scored reasonably on the "which pixel belongs to which class" measure (mIoU 0.308) but was nearly broken on the "did it correctly separate individual instruments" measure (0.0017, essentially zero). The cause was a settings mismatch borrowed from a different, larger dataset's conventions, not the model itself — fixing it improved that second score by about 38x immediately, with no retraining needed.

Then: three more attempts, same ceiling. We tried training longer, a different learning-rate schedule, and pasting in extra rare-instrument examples — three separate, reasonable ideas. All three landed in the same narrow range (mIoU 0.309–0.321). When three different fixes all hit the identical wall, that's a sign the model itself has a hard limit, not that it just needs more training time or a better recipe.

What the wall actually was

A configuration setting meant to keep the output cheap and fine-grained had an unintended side effect: it caused a software utility to cut off the network's deeper layers entirely, not merely leave them unused — they were structurally absent, not just untrained. Every one of the four stuck runs was training a model that physically could not see its own richest, most detailed features, only the shallow, low-detail ones. We confirmed this directly by inspecting the model's internals: the deeper layers genuinely had zero connection to anything being trained.

Fix and final result

We rebuilt the backbone so it actually uses both its shallow and deep features (still small — 5.9M parameters, well under the 18.9M-parameter detector), double-checked it was wired correctly this time, and trained it two ways: on its own, and combined with the rare-instrument-pasting trick from before. Both training runs stopped automatically once they stopped improving. Result: mIoU 0.637 on its own, 0.654 combined with pasting — roughly double the old 0.32 ceiling that four separate attempts couldn't get past.

5.7.1Trying the same tracking trick here

Same question as §5.6, but for this pixel-coloring model instead of the box detector: does bridging a brief gap in tracking recover instruments the model missed in a single frame? To reuse the same tracker, we shrink each predicted instrument shape down to its bounding box just for the tracker's bookkeeping; when a gap gets bridged, we bring back that instrument's last real shape unchanged (a stale guess, same idea as the stale box in §5.6). Tested on the full 2861-instance test set, using the best segmentation checkpoint from above.

Same result as detection, plus a hard limit on what tracking can ever fix

The main instance-finding score dropped (0.380 → 0.351, from stale bridged shapes turning into wrong guesses), while finding heavily-overlapping instruments improved a bit (0.185 → 0.199) — the exact same tradeoff as detection's tracking result in §5.6, again not a clear win. There's also a more basic limit here: tracking can only ever change which individual instances get counted, never the mIoU score, because mIoU comes purely from "which class does this pixel belong to," decided fresh on every single frame with no memory of other frames at all. If we want a meaningfully higher mIoU, tracking is the wrong lever — we'd have to improve the pixel-classifying part of the model itself.

5.7.2Building a stronger model for finding individual instruments (in progress)

Our pixel-coloring model is just the wrong shape of architecture for this particular score. It separates instruments by finding rough "center points" on a coarse grid, which is fine for coloring pixels but bad for drawing crisp, precise outlines around each instrument — and precise outlines are exactly what this score checks. Every published result with a strong score here, TAPIS included, instead does it the way our own box detector already does: draw a box first, then color in just the pixels inside that box. We're already good at the box part (0.831, only 5–9 points off), so instead of training a whole new model from zero, we're taking our trained box detector and bolting a new pixel-coloring piece onto it, keeping the box part exactly as it was and training only the new piece.

A mistake we caught before it gave us a fake result, and an honest expectation

Our first attempt at this reused a box detector that had, unnoticed, already trained on some of the exact same video we planned to test on — like grading a student on questions they'd already seen the answers to. It gave itself away immediately: a suspiciously perfect near-100% score after just one round of training was the tell, not a real result. We fixed it by training a fresh box detector that has genuinely never seen the test video, and we're now training the pixel-coloring piece on top of that instead. Still running, no result yet as of this writing. One honest expectation set in advance, not after the fact: even if this works exactly as hoped, we don't expect to land within a few points of TAPIS's 89.85 — that's a much bigger, more specialized model built by the people who made this dataset. This is still the right thing to try, just not a guaranteed fix for a 52-point gap.

§6 Discussion

6.1Key findings

  1. Letting the small model adjust to this dataset (fine-tuning) beats using it straight off-the-shelf, by a lot (macro-F1 0.506 to 0.645).
  2. Rebalancing rare classes and adding data augmentation each help on their own, and help even more combined, because they fix different problems (macro-F1 0.645 to 0.708).
  3. Model speed on the GPU doesn't track model size the way you'd expect; speed on CPU does.
  4. The big classification model (ResNet-50) does score higher, but the accuracy gain is modest next to how much slower and heavier it is — a real tradeoff, not a model that's simply better in every way.
  5. Cropping to one instrument at a time, instead of asking "what's in this whole frame," fixes almost every remaining weak spot — using the exact same small model.
  6. Our small box detector (mAP@50 = 0.831) beats the big one on accuracy and is 3.1x faster — more capacity actively hurt here, not helped.
  7. The detection stage's real headline finding is a diagnosis, not a score: every version we tried loses 25–30 points of recall specifically on heavily-overlapping instruments, no matter the backbone, loss weighting, or augmentation used.
  8. Tracking helps find overlapping instruments but doesn't raise overall accuracy, even after fixing two real bugs and tuning it. A fancier version (checking for a plausible occluder) didn't help either.
  9. The segmentation model's stuck-at-0.32 plateau turned out to be a real, previously-unnoticed bug, not a training or data problem. Fixing it nearly doubled the score (to 0.654), though it didn't close the much bigger gap on the instance-separation score (§5.7).
  10. Tracking shows the exact same up-a-little/down-a-little tradeoff on this model too, and reveals a hard limit: tracking can only ever affect instance-level scores, never the pixel-classification score.
  11. The 52-point instance-separation gap comes down to using the wrong kind of model, not needing more tuning — our pixel-coloring approach is a poor match for that specific score. Swapping in a box-then-mask approach, reusing our already-good box detector, is the in-progress fix.

6.2How we compare to TAPIS and other published work

TAPIS reports one overall instance-segmentation score, 89.85%, with no per-instrument breakdown. Our Task A/B classification numbers aren't a fair comparison to that figure — they're a different, easier kind of problem. Our box-detection score is a fair, apples-to-apples comparison (same metric family TAPIS and other published work use), and we're 5–9 points short there. Our finished segmentation work gives two more honest, apples-to-apples numbers: 0.654 vs. a comparable published 80.07 (15 points short) on the pixel-classification score, and 0.380 vs. TAPIS's 89.85 (52 points short) on the instance-separation score. That second, much bigger gap isn't a mystery — we traced it to using the wrong kind of model for that particular score (§5.7.2), and we're addressing it now, without pretending we'll definitely close it.

§7 Limitations

  • Task B's number assumes we already know exactly where each instrument is (it uses the true outline, not a predicted one). It measures "can the model name an instrument once it's found," not the accuracy of a full find-and-name pipeline.
  • Rare instruments (Clip Applier: 102 instances total; Laparoscopic Grasper: 275) don't give us a lot of examples to be confident about, statistically.
  • Only 13 real surgeries exist in this whole dataset (8 for training, 5 for testing) — real variation between different surgeries and surgeons isn't well captured by just one split like this.
  • Our overlapping-instruments check is based on box overlap, not actual pixel-level hiding — two boxes can overlap on paper without one instrument really covering the other.
  • The pixel-coloring model's instance-level scores are computed on a shrunk-down version of its output, not the full-resolution image — the real mask quality is probably a bit better than these numbers suggest.
  • The box-then-mask model (§5.7.2) is still training as of this writing; no final number yet.
  • We didn't measure CPU speed or a couple of standard size metrics for the box-drawing or pixel-coloring models — they're less reliable to estimate for these more complex model types, so we chose not to report made-up numbers.
  • Our literature comparison came from web searches, not a formal, exhaustive review process.

§8 Future work

(1) Finish the box-then-mask model and report its real, cross-checked score honestly against TAPIS, whatever it turns out to be. (2) If the gap is still wide even with the right kind of model, that's a legitimate place to stop chasing bigger architecture changes and call detection + tracking + a well-understood (if not fully closed) segmentation result the project's main contribution. (3) A final round of speed/size optimization and one combined chart showing every model in this report on the same accuracy-vs-cost picture.
