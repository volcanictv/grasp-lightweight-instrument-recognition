\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Robot-assisted surgery produces hours of endoscopic video, and analysing it as it is recorded would support context-aware assistance, skill assessment and, further out, partial automation of surgical steps \citep{ayobi2024pixel}.
These uses share a visual foundation: which instruments are in view, where they are, and which kind each one is.
Instrument recognition, the segmentation of every instrument together with its type, is therefore a basic building block of surgical scene understanding \citep{gonzalez2020isinet,ayobi2023matis}, and for live assistance it must work online: it can only use frames that have already arrived, and it must keep up with the video.

Naming the type of an instrument from a single frame is hard.
Instruments of different types share shafts, wrists and jaws, and the parts that tell them apart are often out of frame, occluded or turned away from the camera.
Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows a typical case: a Large Needle Driver whose keyframe view is classified as a Bipolar Forceps (belief 0.47), while earlier frames show the instrument clearly and are classified correctly.
Looking back resolves such cases, but it is expensive.
It requires following the instrument through the video and classifying it again on every frame, and most instruments do not need it.
For an online system the question is therefore not whether to track but which instruments to track.

The strongest published system on the GraSP benchmark, TAPIS \citep{ayobi2024pixel}, detects and classifies instruments with a Mask2Former detector \citep{cheng2022mask2former} and a video transformer that reads a time window centred on the keyframe.
It therefore needs future frames, which a live system does not have, and it spends the same temporal budget on every instrument without an uncertainty estimate to say where the budget is needed.
Annotated frames are few and the classes imbalanced, the rarest instrument having about 25 times fewer examples than the most common, so models should start from strong pretrained representations.
Promptable segmenters supply them for masks: given a box, SAM2 and SAM3 return a mask \citep{ravi2024sam2,carion2025sam3}, but not the instrument's type.

We make tracking evidence-based.
A given box prompts fine-tuned SAM2 and SAM3 segmenters, and a light four-member evidential ensemble classifies the masked crop in one pass, returning the class and an epistemic uncertainty \citep{sensoy2018evidential,duan2024evidential}.
Only instruments whose evidence is weak are followed backward through past frames, and the per-frame predictions are fused by confidence (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
We assume the boxes are given: instrument detectors are already strong (TAPIS reports 89.85 box mAP at an IoU of 0.5 on this data), so we study recognition given boxes and leave the integration of a detector to future work (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

On GraSP, tracking the 19\% of instruments with the weakest evidence recovers 87\% of the accuracy gain of tracking the weakest 31\%, and the resulting causal pipeline reaches 84.2 mIoU, 82.3 IoU and 71.9 mcIoU (Section~\ref{sec:gate}).
Its non-causal counterpart, an upper bound that also reads future frames, reaches 87.4, 86.2 and 78.3 against 86.6, 83.4 and 77.4 for TAPIS, clearly above it on IoU (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
The evidential score detects errors as well as MC dropout with one forward pass instead of 80 and, unlike a vote, flags errors on which all members agree (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty}).
